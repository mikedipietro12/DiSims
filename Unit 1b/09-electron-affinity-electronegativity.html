      IE of F⁻

      F + energy → F⁺ + e⁻ = ionization energy

      F + e⁻ → F⁻ + energy

      F⁻ + energy → F + e⁻ = EA

      The energy released when F gains an electron is the same magnitude as the energy needed to take it back off — the ionization energy of the anion. That is how electron affinity is measured. A stronger affinity means a more stable anion — and a harder electron to pull away.

      All of these have an electron affinity of zero — can you hypothesize why?

      Mg and Ar both have filled subshells (Mg ends in 3s²; Ar ends in a closed 3p⁶ shell). Adding an extra electron would mean opening a new subshell that isn’t occupied yet — so neither atom binds that electron into a stable anion.

      N is different: its 2p subshell is half-full, with three electrons of the same spin. Forcing a fourth electron into that arrangement creates electron repulsion and disrupts the stable half-filled set — again, no stable anion forms.

      EA · PubChem

      Pick two atoms that can share a pair. Watch who pulls harder.

      Higher core charge pulls the shared pair closer — and when core charge matches, the smaller atom wins the tug. That pull is electronegativity. The pair sits unevenly: one end slightly negative (δ−), the other slightly positive (δ+).
