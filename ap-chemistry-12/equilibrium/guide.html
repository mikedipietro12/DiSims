Guide

Some changes give off heat. Some spread things out. Many reactions run both ways at once. This guide names each idea, shows how temperature decides which way a reaction goes, and ends by building the equilibrium constant, K, from rates.

All the way, or stuck partway?

Up until this point in chemistry, we've assumed that all reactions go to completion, favouring the products. We've thought of reversible reactions only as physical changes.

In this lesson, we'll start to discover the energetic forces that cause some systems to react fully, and others to get stuck in equilibrium.

  • Watch the first two: the reactants are used up, and the products never turn back.
  • Watch the last two: particles keep crossing both ways, but the amounts on each side stay the same.

Up the hill, then down

You already know this one. Enthalpy (H) is the heat stored in a system. ΔH is how much it changes.

A potential energy diagram follows a reaction from left to right. To react, particles must collide hard enough to climb to the top of the hill, the activated complex. Past the top, they slide down to the products.

ΔH is the gap between where the reactants start and where the products end. If the products end lower, that energy leaves as heat and warms the surroundings. ΔH is negative: the change is exothermic. If the products end higher, the energy comes in as heat from the surroundings. ΔH is positive: the change is endothermic.

  • Watch: in Exothermic, A₂ and B₂ climb together. At the top, bonds break and new ones form, so the atoms re-pair as AB. Heat comes off as the products settle on the lower shelf.
  • Switch to Endothermic: the same hill, read backwards. AB re-pairs into A₂ and B₂, and heat flows in.
  • Notice: Eₐ and ΔH are different gaps. Eₐ is about how fast. ΔH is about how much heat.

Where does the heat go?

Now we need to think about actual systems, and the flow of heat energy into or out of the system.

The sign tells us part of the story, but combined with our understanding of the PE diagrams, we can get a full picture of what is going on.

Recall: breaking bonds or pulling particles apart costs energy. Forming bonds or letting particles settle into a lower energy state releases energy.

  • ΔH + when more energy goes into breaking than comes out of forming. Melting and boiling are examples.
  • ΔH − when forming wins. Burning fuel and freezing are examples.
  • Watch: what changes in each demo? Use it to decide which way heat flows, then predict the sign of ΔH.
  • Heat is a term in the equation: when heat comes out, it is written on the products side. When heat goes in, it is written on the reactants side.

Physical changes like melting and chemical changes like burning both have a ΔH. The rule is the same for both.

Let's talk about randomness

One of the ways we can talk about entropy is thinking about randomness. Or, things "spreading out". Think about dropping food colouring into water. It spreads out until the colour is even. It never gathers itself back into the drop. Entropy (S) measures how spread out, or random, a system's particles and energy are. The more random ways the particles can be arranged, the higher the entropy.

ΔS is the change. Spreading out makes ΔS positive. Getting more ordered or more crowded makes ΔS negative.

  • Solid → liquid → gas: each step makes ΔS more positive. A gas has by far the most freedom.
  • More gas particles: a reaction that makes more gas particles has ΔS +. N₂O₄ → 2NO₂ turns one particle into two.
  • Dissolving: usually ΔS +, as a neat crystal breaks up into ions that spread through the water.
  • Watch: the same particles before and after. Think about how many ways they could be arranged.

Rules of thumb have exceptions. Ca²⁺ is a small ion with a big charge, so it attracts water molecules strongly and binds them in a tight shell. Those trapped water molecules lose more freedom than the ions gain. Chemists check the data rather than trust the rule.

Which side is favoured?

Every reaction is written with reactants on the left and products on the right. When we say ΔH or ΔS favours the products, we mean that, on its own, it pushes the reaction forward (→). Favours the reactants means it pushes the reaction in reverse (←).

Each term has its own rule for which side it favours.

  • Enthalpy favours the side with lower potential energy. ΔH − means the products store less potential energy in their bonds and attractions than the reactants. Going forward, the difference is released to the surroundings as heat, so the products are favoured. ΔH + means the products store more potential energy, so heat must be absorbed from the surroundings to form them, and the reactants are favoured.
  • Entropy favours the side that is more spread out. ΔS + means the products have more ways to be arranged: the products are favoured. ΔS − means the reactants are more spread out, so the reactants are favoured.
  • Watch: switch between the four cases under the picture. The bold half of ⇌ points at the side that term favours.

"Favoured" is one push, not the final answer. A reaction feels both pushes at once, which is where we go next.

Two different pulls

ΔH and ΔS are not the same thing. ΔH is about heat. ΔS is about randomness. A change is favoured when it gives off heat (ΔH −), and also when a system gets more random, or spread out (ΔS +).

We will now look at systems and decide which direction entropy and enthalpy are "pointing", or which side is favoured. A cold pack fights. It takes in heat, which works against it, yet it still dissolves on its own. Spreading out wins.

  • Both favour it (ΔH −, ΔS +): it happens.
  • Both oppose it (ΔH +, ΔS −): it does not happen on its own.
  • They fight: the winner depends on temperature. We will get to that with ΔG.
  • Notice: step through the cold pack with the arrows under the picture. Watch which way each half of the ⇌ points.

Four ways the pulls can line up

Chemists combine ΔH and ΔS into one quantity, the Gibbs free energy change:

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

If ΔG is negative, the products are favoured.

If ΔG is positive, the reactants are favoured.

T is the temperature in kelvin, which is always positive.

Notice the minus sign: when ΔS is +, the entropy term (−TΔS) is negative, so it helps make ΔG negative.

Reactions that run both ways

Many reactions are reversible. As soon as products form, some of them react back into reactants. We write this with a double arrow: ⇌.

Here is N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ in a sealed vessel. N₂O₄ is colourless and NO₂ is brown. Watch the colour (what you would see in the lab), then watch the particles (what you would see if you could zoom in).

  • Macro: the colour darkens, then holds steady.
  • Micro: particles keep splitting and joining. They never stop.
  • Watch: each ring is one reaction event. Count them after the colour stops changing.

The bottle looks finished, but it is not. Both reactions are still happening at the same speed.

When the rates meet

At the start there is only N₂O₄, so the forward reaction is fast and the reverse is zero. As NO₂ builds up, the forward rate slows (fewer N₂O₄ left to split) and the reverse rate speeds up (more NO₂ to join).

Eventually the two rates are equal. From then on, every NO₂ made is matched by one used up. The amounts stop changing even though both reactions continue. This is dynamic equilibrium.

  • Amount graph: the lines curve, then go flat.
  • Rate graph: the forward line falls and the reverse line rises until they meet.
  • Equilibrium does not mean equal amounts. It means equal rates.
  • Try: start with products instead. The rates still meet, from the other side.

You already know rates from collision theory. Equilibrium is what happens when two opposite rates balance.

Equilibrium is all around you

A sealed soda bottle is a system at equilibrium. CO₂ gas above the drink dissolves into the liquid at the same rate as dissolved CO₂ escapes. The fizz stays put. Open the cap and the system is no longer closed, so the gas escapes faster than it returns.

The same balance shows up far from the chemistry lab.

  • Your blood: four linked equilibria, from CO₂(g) in your lungs to H⁺ and CO₃²⁻ in your blood. Each breath in removes a little CO₂ and shifts them all left; each breath out lets CO₂ build up and shifts them right. A panic attack blows off so much CO₂ that the blood becomes less acidic. Exercise adds H⁺ and pushes the blood the other way.
  • The ocean: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq) at the surface, then CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ in the water. More CO₂ in the air means more H⁺ in the sea: ocean acidification.
  • Notice: each one needs a closed system and two opposite processes running at the same rate.
  • Try: change a condition under the picture, watch the two arrows in the equation pull apart, then settle to equal again. Change it back and the system returns.

Where else might two opposite processes balance? Think about population in an ecosystem, or water evaporating and raining back down.

Only the start and finish matter

Climb a mountain by the steep trail or the long switchbacks. Either way your change in height is the same, because it depends only on where you started and where you finished. The distance you walked is different for each route.

H, S and G are state functions. Like height, their change depends only on the start and end states, not the path. That is why Hess's law works: add up any route of steps and you get the same ΔH. Heat and work are not state functions. They depend on the path, like the distance walked.

  • State functions: H, S, G (and height, temperature, pressure).
  • Path-dependent: heat, work (and distance walked).
  • Watch: two different routes between the same start and end give the same Δ.

Temperature picks the winner

Combine the two pulls into one number, Gibbs free energy:

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

If ΔG is negative, the reaction goes forward on its own; we call it spontaneous. If ΔG is positive, the reverse is favoured. The T in the middle is the temperature in kelvin. The hotter it gets, the more the entropy pull (−TΔS) counts.

  • The bars: ΔH stays put. −TΔS stretches as you drag T. ΔG is where they land together.
  • When the pulls fight, dragging T across a certain point flips ΔG's sign.
  • Try: N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ (ΔH +, ΔS +). Cold, ΔG is +; hot, ΔG is −.
  • Try: Haber (ΔH −, ΔS −). It flips the other way.

"Spontaneous" does not mean fast or sudden. It only means "goes this way without being pushed". See the next beat.

What the formula leaves out

ΔG is powerful, but it is a model, and every model has limits.

It says nothing about speed. Diamond turning into graphite has ΔG −, so it is spontaneous at every temperature. But at room temperature it is so slow that it has never been observed: the hill between diamond and graphite is enormous. ΔG tells you which way a reaction can go. Rates (and collision theory) tell you how fast.

It assumes ΔH and ΔS stay fixed. Real values drift as temperature changes, so the flip temperature you find with ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is an estimate, not an exact answer.

  • Which way: ΔG.
  • How fast: rates and activation energy.
  • Watch: the carbon atoms climb part way up the hill and slide back, while the clock runs to billions of years.
  • Heat it (no air): now atoms get over the hill and settle as graphite. ΔG didn't change sign. Only the rate did.
  • Notice: a "spontaneous" reaction can still need a spark to get started (think of a match).

Can you think of another reaction that "should" happen but doesn't seem to? (Paper burning in air is one.)

A puzzle about rate laws

You learned that a rate law has to be found by experiment. Take 2NO₂ + F₂ → 2NO₂F. The coefficients suggest rate = k[NO₂]²[F₂], but experiments give rate = k[NO₂][F₂]. It is first order in NO₂, not second. The coefficients don't work for kinetics.

Yet the equilibrium constant for the same reaction is K = [NO₂F]² / [NO₂]²[F₂], with every coefficient back as an exponent. Why does equilibrium get to use the coefficients when kinetics can't?

  • Step through with the arrows under the picture: test the guess, then compare it with K.
  • The answer is about rates. The next sections build it: elementary steps, then K from equal rates, then K for a whole mechanism.

Kinetics asks which road you took. Equilibrium only asks where you ended up.

One step at a time

The route a reaction really takes is its mechanism: a series of simple steps that add up to the balanced equation.

Each step is a single event: one particle breaking apart, or two particles colliding. These are elementary steps. For an elementary step, and only for an elementary step, you can write the rate law straight from the equation. Each reactant's coefficient becomes its exponent.

  • One particle (A → products): rate = k[A].
  • Two different particles (A + B → products): rate = k[A][B].
  • Two of the same (2A → products): rate = k[A][A] = k[A]².
  • Try: double the amount of A in each case. Which one makes the rate go up four times? Why?

Only the reactants of a step appear in its rate law. The products don't change how fast that step happens.

Equal rates make a constant

Back to N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂. Both directions are elementary steps: one N₂O₄ breaks apart, or two NO₂ collide and join. So each direction has its own rate law, with its own rate constant: kf forward and kr reverse.

At equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal. Set the two rate laws equal, rearrange, and out comes the equilibrium constant, K:

K = [NO₂]²[N₂O₄]

  • Step through with the arrows under the picture. Watch where each term goes.
  • Why it's constant: kf and kr are each fixed at a given temperature, so kf ÷ kr is fixed too. That ratio is K.
  • The pattern: products on top, reactants on the bottom, each coefficient becomes an exponent.
  • Notice: the amounts at equilibrium depend on where you start, but this ratio of them always comes out to the same K.
  • Temperature changes kf and kr, so it changes K.

K and ΔG tell the same story. K bigger than 1 (more products at equilibrium) goes with ΔG negative. K smaller than 1 goes with ΔG positive.

Add the steps, multiply the K's

Back to the puzzle: 2NO₂ + F₂ ⇌ 2NO₂F. It happens in two elementary steps. Step 1 is slow: NO₂ + F₂ ⇌ NO₂F + F. Step 2 is fast: NO₂ + F ⇌ NO₂F. The slow step limits the speed, which is why the measured rate law, k[NO₂][F₂], is step 1's own rate law.

Add the steps and F cancels: it is made in step 1 and used up in step 2. F is an intermediate. At equilibrium every step is at equilibrium, so each step has its own K. Multiply them, and [F] cancels in exactly the same way.

  • Adding equations means multiplying K's: K = K₁ × K₂.
  • Whatever cancels in the equations cancels in K.
  • What's left matches the overall equation: the two [NO₂F] make [NO₂F]², and the two [NO₂] make [NO₂]². The coefficients come back as exponents.
  • Last step: double the equation and K is squared. The rate law doesn't change at all. A rate law and K are different kinds of things.

Challenge: write K for 2NO₂F ⇌ 2NO₂ + F₂, the same reaction written backwards. How does it compare with K for the forward direction?

The law of mass action

Part 4 comes down to one rule. For a balanced equation at equilibrium, K is the products over the reactants, each raised to the power of its coefficient. This is the law of mass action:

K = [C]c[D]d[A]a[B]b

  • Products on top, reactants on the bottom.
  • Coefficients become exponents. This works for the overall equation, whatever the mechanism, because at equilibrium every step is at equilibrium.
  • The value depends on the reaction and the temperature. N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ has K = 4.63 × 10⁻³ at 25 °C: much smaller than 1, so at equilibrium the mixture is mostly N₂O₄.

K is fixed at a given temperature. Change the temperature and K changes.

Change the equation, change K

K belongs to an equation exactly as it is written. Change the equation and K changes in a predictable way.

  • Step through with the arrows under the picture.
  • Add equations, multiply their K's: K = K₁ × K₂. Whatever cancels in the equations cancels in K.
  • Reverse an equation, take 1/K: products and reactants swap places, so the fraction flips over. 1 ÷ (4.63 × 10⁻³) = 216.
  • Notice: the small K becomes a big K′. The mixture at equilibrium is the same. Written backwards, N₂O₄ is now the product, and the equation favours it.

You met one more on the Overall K beat: double an equation and K is squared. In general, multiply an equation by n and K is raised to the power n.

Leave out solids and pure liquids

So far, every substance in our equations has been a gas. Many reactions also have a solid or a liquid in them, so we need one more rule before we write K.

  • The rule: solids (s) and pure liquids (l) are left out of K. Gases (g) and dissolved substances (aq) stay in.
  • Why: a pure solid's concentration is set by how tightly its particles are packed, so it never changes. Add more CaCO₃ and you add more litres of it too. A number that never changes is folded into K.
  • Pure liquids: the same idea. That includes water when it is the solvent. It is about 55.5 mol/L, and the reaction barely changes that.
  • Check the phase, not the formula: H₂O(l) is left out, but H₂O(g) stays in. As a gas, its concentration changes as it reacts, like any other gas.

In the simulation, click the arrows under the picture to watch each one drop out of K.

The solids still have to be there. With no CaCO₃ or no CaO in the container, there is no equilibrium. They just don't appear in K.

Big K, small K

K is products over reactants, measured at equilibrium. So the size of K tells you what the equilibrium mixture is mostly made of. Take the simplest reaction, A ⇌ B, where K is just B over A:

K = [B][A]

  • Pick a K and watch the box settle. The bar shows the amounts now. The tick shows where they end up.
  • K bigger than 1: more products than reactants at equilibrium. The equilibrium favours products. At K = 1000 there are about 1000 B for every A, so the reaction nearly goes to completion.
  • K smaller than 1: more reactants than products. The equilibrium favours reactants. At K = 0.001 the reaction barely goes.
  • K = 1: equal amounts. Neither side is favoured.
  • Start with all B and keep the same K. The box ends up at the same mix. K sets where it ends up, wherever it starts.

The size of K says where a reaction ends up, not how fast it gets there. A huge K can still be slow, just like diamond turning into graphite.

What to take with you

  • ΔH is heat flow. ΔS is spreading out. They are different pulls.
  • Many reactions run both ways. At equilibrium the rates are equal, and both directions keep going.
  • ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Temperature decides who wins when the pulls fight.
  • ΔG tells you which way, not how fast.
  • Kinetics asks which road you took. Equilibrium only asks where you ended up. At equilibrium every elementary step is balanced, so K comes from the overall coefficients even when the rate law doesn't.
  • For an elementary step, coefficients become exponents in the rate law. For an overall equation, you need experiments.
  • At equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal, which gives K: products over reactants, coefficients as exponents. That is the law of mass action.
  • Adding equations multiplies their K's. Reversing an equation gives 1/K.
  • Solids (s) and pure liquids (l) are left out of K. Gases (g) and dissolved substances (aq) stay in.
  • K bigger than 1: products are favoured at equilibrium. K smaller than 1: reactants are favoured.
