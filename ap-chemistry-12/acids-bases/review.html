Part 1.1: What Makes an Acid an Acid?

The Arrhenius Theory

Last year, we learned the first theory of acids and bases, from Svante Arrhenius. We will start there, since it still works for most of what we do.

Acid: releases H+ in water.
Base: releases OH− in water.
Salt: an ionic compound that is neither an acid nor a base. It is what is left after an acid and a base neutralize each other.

A quick way to tell: an acid's formula starts with H, a base's formula ends with OH, and a salt is ionic but does neither. Watch for exceptions: CH3OH ends with OH, but it is not ionic, so it is not a base.

An acid: HCl is a molecule. When it dissolves, the pair of electrons in the H–Cl bond stays with Cl. Therefore H leaves as H+, and Cl becomes Cl−.
A base: NaOH is ionic. In water, its Na+ and OH− ions separate.

Neutralization

When an acid and a base react, they neutralize each other.

Acid + base → salt + water

Recall: every neutralization comes down to the same reaction: H+ + OH− → H2O. Therefore we balance one by matching the H+ and the OH− first.

Properties: before anyone knew about H+ and OH−, acids and bases were sorted by how they behave: their colour on litmus, their taste, how they feel, and whether they react with metals.

To balance a neutralization:

  1. Count the H+ the acid can release and the OH− the base can release.
  2. Put the smallest coefficients in front so there are as many H+ as OH−.
  3. Write one H2O for each H+ and OH−.
  4. The ions that are left make the salt, positive ion first.

For example, with HNO3 and Sr(OH)2: two HNO3 match the two OH−, so two waters form, and Sr2+ with two NO3− make Sr(NO3)2. Sr2+ and NO3− are spectators, which leaves the net ionic equation H+ + OH− → H2O.

In the simulation, click the “Next step” button to watch the ions come together as we balance it. Then, in the “Properties” tab, decide which properties belong to acids, bases, or both.

What Is H+, Really?

A hydrogen atom is one proton and one electron. If it loses its electron, only the proton is left, so H+ is called the proton.

A bare proton has its whole +1 charge packed into a tiny space, so it is strongly attracted to the lone pairs on a water molecule. In water, every H+ bonds to a water molecule to make H3O+, the hydronium ion.

Therefore H+(aq) and H3O+(aq) mean the same thing. When we write an acid in water, we add H2O to both sides, and the H+ and H2O on the right join as H3O+:
HCl(g) → H+(aq) + Cl−(aq) becomes HCl(g) + H2O(l) → H3O+(aq) + Cl−(aq).

The Brønsted-Lowry Theory

The Arrhenius theory did not account for equilibrium reactions. The Brønsted-Lowry theory includes everything Arrhenius said, and adds reactions that run both ways.

Acid: a proton donor. It loses an H+.
Base: a proton acceptor. It gains an H+.

Both directions: the reaction is an equilibrium, so the proton also moves back. That means each side has an acid and a base. In NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4+ + OH−, H2O is the acid and NH3 the base on the left; NH4+ is the acid and OH− the base on the right.

The trick: find the similar species on the other side of the equation. If it has one more H, the reactant was a base. If it has one less H, the reactant was an acid.

Amphiprotic: can act as an acid or a base, depending on what it reacts with. Water is the main example. Negative ions that still have an H to lose, such as HS− and HCO3−, are amphiprotic too.

Polyprotic: can supply more than one proton. A monoprotic acid supplies one (HCl), a diprotic acid up to two (H2SO4), a triprotic acid up to three (H3PO4).

In “Amphiprotic and polyprotic”, watch HS− act as an acid and then as a base, then see a monoprotic, a diprotic and a triprotic acid release their protons one at a time. Then try “Check yourself”.

Conjugate Acids and Bases

In a Brønsted-Lowry reaction, the proton moves back and forth. Each time it moves, the acid that lost it becomes a base, and the base that gained it becomes an acid.

Conjugate pair: two species that differ by one proton.
Conjugate acid: the member of the pair that has the extra proton.
Conjugate base: the member of the pair that lacks it.

For example: NH4+ and NH3 are a conjugate pair. NH4+ is the conjugate acid and NH3 is the conjugate base.

In the simulation, match each species with its conjugate: click one, then the one that is one H+ different. A correct pair locks in and shows which is the conjugate acid and which is the conjugate base.

Strong and Weak

Strong: 100% ionized in water.
Weak: less than 100% ionized in water. Most of a weak acid stays as whole molecules.

Strong and weak are not the same as concentrated and dilute. Strong or weak is how much of the acid ionizes. Concentrated or dilute is how many moles are in each litre.

Find them in the Data Booklet (page 6): the strong acids are the top six on the left, with one-way arrows. The common strong bases are the metal hydroxides. NH3 is a weak base.

Levelling: in water, all six strong acids are 100% ionized into H3O+. Therefore they all have the same strength in water, and H3O+ is the strongest acid that can exist in water.

Self-Ionization of Water

In Part 1.1, we saw water act as a base with HF and as an acid with NH3. Water can also do both at once, with another water molecule.

Self-ionization: one water molecule passes a proton to another. At any moment, this happens to only a tiny fraction of the molecules.
H2O(l) + 59 kJ ⇌ H+(aq) + OH−(aq)

Kw: the equilibrium constant for this reaction. [H2O] is a constant, so it is left out of the expression.
Kw = [H+][OH−] = 1.00 × 10−14 at 25 °C
Written with hydronium, this is Kw = [H3O+][OH−]. In this Part, we write H+ to keep the expressions short.

Neutral: [H+] = [OH−]. In pure water, both are 1.0 × 10−7 M.
Acidic: [H+] > [OH−].
Basic: [H+] < [OH−].

Heating water: the reaction is endothermic, so heating shifts it to the right. That means Kw gets bigger, but [H+] and [OH−] stay equal, so hot pure water is still neutral. From now on, we assume 25 °C unless we are told otherwise.

In the simulation, click the “Next step” button to watch one water molecule pass a proton to another. Then try “Hot water”.

The Balance

Kw is fixed, so [H+] and [OH−] are tied together. When one goes up, the other must come down by the same factor, so their product stays 1.00 × 10−14.

Adding an acid: [H+] goes up. The extra H+ reacts with OH− to make water, so [OH−] goes down. It never reaches zero: even an acidic solution has a little OH−.
Adding a base: [OH−] goes up, and [H+] goes down.

Notice: a weak acid or base at the same concentration moves the balance much less, because only a small part of it ionizes.

Watch the two sides of the Kw expression grow and shrink while their product stays the same. The bars show each concentration on a powers-of-ten scale. The particle picture is scaled down, but the numbers are exact.

Finding One From the Other

Since [H+][OH−] = 1.00 × 10−14, knowing one concentration gives us the other:
[OH−] = Kw ÷ [H+] and [H+] = Kw ÷ [OH−]

Strong acids: 100% ionized, so [H+] is the acid's concentration.
Strong bases: 100% ionized, so [OH−] is the base's concentration times the number of OH− in its formula. Sr(OH)2 gives two OH− for each formula unit.

Recall: to go from grams to moles to mol/L, we use unit analysis, as we did in the Solubility unit.

pH and pOH

Concentrations of H+ and OH− are often tiny, so we use their powers of ten instead.

pH: pH = −log[H+]
pOH: pOH = −log[OH−]

Logs: the log of a number is its power of ten. log(1000) = 3 and log(0.01) = −2. To go back, we take the antilog: [H+] = 10−pH.

A factor of ten: when the pH goes up by 1, [H+] goes down by 10 times.
The scale: at 25 °C, an acidic solution has a pH below 7, a neutral one 7, and a basic one above 7.

Significant figures: in a pH, only the digits after the decimal are significant. They come from the number in front of the power of ten. The digits before the decimal only come from the power of ten.
For example, [H+] = 4.7 × 10−4 M has 2 significant figures, so its pH has 2 decimal places: pH = 3.33.

In the simulation, drag the slider along the pH scale and watch [H+] and [OH−] change by powers of ten. Then look at “Significant figures”.

The Magic Square

[H+], [OH−], pH and pOH sit at the four corners of a square. Given any one of them, we can find the other three.

Top: [H+][OH−] = 1.00 × 10−14
Bottom: pH + pOH = 14.00
Left: pH = −log[H+], and back again with [H+] = 10−pH
Right: pOH = −log[OH−], and back again with [OH−] = 10−pOH

In the simulation, type a value into any corner and press Enter: the square works out the other three corners and shows each step. Or click the “Next step” button to travel around the square one corner at a time, starting from [OH−] = 3.2 × 10−5 M. Then try “Acidic or basic?”.

Mixing a Strong Acid and a Strong Base

When we mix a strong acid and a strong base, H3O+ and OH− react to make water until one of them runs out:
H3O+ + OH− → 2 H2O

The excess: whichever ion is left over decides the pH. If H3O+ is left over, the mixture is acidic. If OH− is left over, it is basic.

The moles route:

  1. Find the moles of H3O+ and the moles of OH−.
  2. Subtract the smaller from the larger to find the moles left over.
  3. Divide by the total volume of the mixture to find the concentration.
  4. Find the pH (or the pOH first, if OH− is left over).

Notice: the volume is both solutions added together. Mixing dilutes both of them.

In the simulation, click the “Next step” button to find the moles under each beaker, then pour them together: the mixture fills to the total volume. The two amounts of moles move over to the mixture, where the smaller is taken from the larger to find what is left over.

Two OH− per Formula

Sr(OH)2 releases two OH− for each formula unit:
Sr(OH)2(s) → Sr2+(aq) + 2 OH−(aq)

Therefore the moles of OH− are twice the moles of Sr(OH)2. The 2 goes into the unit analysis as a mole ratio. The rest is the same as before.

If OH− is left over: find [OH−], then the pOH, then pH = 14.000 − pOH.

In the simulation, click the “Next step” button to build the calculation under each beaker. Count the OH− in the base's beaker: there are two for every Sr2+.

Working Backwards

Sometimes we know the pH we want, and need to find how much acid or base will get us there.

The plan:

  1. From the target pH, find the [H3O+] that will be left over, then its moles.
  2. Find the moles of H3O+ we start with.
  3. The difference is the moles that must react, so that is the moles of OH− to add.
  4. Turn those moles into grams of the base.

Assume: adding a small mass of solid does not change the volume.

In the simulation, click the “Next step” button to work through the example. Then try “Your turn”.

The Glassware

A titration measures how much of one solution exactly reacts with a known volume of another. One of the two has an unknown concentration.

Burette: delivers a measured volume of the titrant. Rinse it with the solution it will hold. Read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level, to 0.01 mL.
Pipette and bulb: measures an exact volume of the sample, such as 25.00 mL, into the flask. Rinse it with the sample first. Never pipette by mouth.
Erlenmeyer flasks: three, one for each trial. Each holds a sample and the indicator, and its shape lets us swirl without spilling. Rinsing a flask with distilled water is fine, because extra water does not change the moles inside.
Waste beakers: two, to catch the rinsings from the burette and the pipette, so nothing goes back into the stock bottles.
Indicator: a few drops change colour at the end of the titration. We use phenolphthalein.
White tile: sits under the flask, so the first faint pink is easy to see.
Wash bottle: distilled water to rinse the burette tip and the walls of the flask.

Standard solution: a solution with an accurately known concentration.
Primary standard: a pure, stable solid with a known molar mass, which we weigh to make a standard solution. The three to know are potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHC8H4O4), oxalic acid dihydrate (H2C2O4·2H2O) and sodium carbonate (Na2CO3).
NaOH is not a primary standard: it absorbs water and CO2 from the air. Therefore we find its exact concentration by titrating it, which standardizes it.

In the simulation, click each piece of glassware to see its name.

The Equivalence Point

Equivalence point: the moles of acid and base exactly match the mole ratio in the balanced equation.
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H2O: 1 mol HCl for every 1 mol NaOH.
H2SO4 + 2 NaOH → Na2SO4 + 2 H2O: 1 mol H2SO4 for every 2 mol NaOH.

End point: where the indicator changes colour. With phenolphthalein, it is the first faint pink that stays when we swirl. We will see why indicators work in Part 3.1.

Notice: pink flashes where the NaOH lands, then fades when we swirl. Near the end point, it takes longer to fade. That is the sign to slow down to one drop at a time.

In the simulation, the titration uses the same solutions as our lab: NaOH in the burette, and 25.00 mL of acid with phenolphthalein in the flask. Open the stopcock to a stream, switch to dropwise near the end, and finish one drop at a time. Swirl after each addition. When the faint pink stays, start a new trial: the burette readings go into the table.

Keyboard: hold S to stream, D for dropwise, F for one drop, hold W to swirl, N for a new trial, and 1 or 2 to choose the acid.

The Titration Calculation

Every titration calculation has three steps, written as unit analysis:

  1. The moles of the solution we know both the volume and the concentration of.
  2. The mole ratio from the balanced equation, to the moles of the other substance.
  3. Those moles divided by the volume of the other solution, for its concentration.

Precision: one trial is not enough. We repeat the titration until at least two trials agree within 0.1 mL. A trial that does not agree is left out, and we average the rest. The first, quick trial is usually the one left out.

In the simulation, click the “Next step” button to build each calculation. The H2SO4 tab shows the 1 : 2 mole ratio.

Sources of Error

In our lab, we find the concentration of the acid from the volume of NaOH we needed.

Too high: anything that makes the NaOH volume we record bigger than it should be.
Too low: anything that makes it smaller, or puts fewer moles of acid in the flask than we think.
No different: anything that changes neither the moles of acid in the flask nor the moles of NaOH that react.

In the simulation, decide what each mistake does to the concentration we calculate for the acid.
