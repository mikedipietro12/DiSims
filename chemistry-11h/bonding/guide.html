Chapter A: From atoms to bonds

How electronegativity and ionization energy decide whether electrons are shared or transferred.

Where we left off

In Unit 1b you built electron configurations from measured data. When we start considering how atoms bond, we only look at the valence electrons. The outermost electrons take part in bonding for both atoms involved.

A Lewis dot symbol keeps just those on display: the element symbol with one dot per valence electron, spread around four sides before any are paired.

Pick an element to watch its valence electrons become a dot symbol.

Two atoms, one shared pair

Two hydrogen atoms each bring one electron. Held between both nuclei, the pair attracts both of them at once. That shared pair is a covalent bond.

Both nuclei pull on it equally, so the cloud of the bond is even. In sharing two electrons, each hydrogen atom has filled its outermost subshell.

Watch two H atoms come together. Replay to see it again.

Electronegativity decides the pull

As we learned in the previous unit, electronegativity (EN) is how strongly an atom pulls bonding electrons toward itself. When the two atoms differ, the shared pair shifts toward the higher-EN atom. We will inspect these trends a bit closer considering the INTRAmolecular forces between two atoms.

From here on, red marks a positive region (δ+) and yellow marks a negative region (δ−).

Pick two elements, or drag the ΔEN slider, and watch the shared pair shift.

A continuum, not boxes

Small EN differences give nonpolar covalent bonds. Larger ones give polar covalent bonds. Very large ones move the electron completely: an ionic bond.

The cut-offs are guides, not walls. Bonds slide smoothly from one type to the next.

Click a bond on the scale to see its cloud.

Real examples: shared

Cl₂ shares evenly. HCl and H₂O share unevenly, so they have δ+ and δ− ends.

Real examples: transferred

When a metal meets a nonmetal, the EN difference is large enough that electrons move across instead of being shared.

Drag each atom in from the tray and click to give it its valence electrons (Na one, Cl seven). Then drag sodium’s electron onto chlorine. Try MgO after, what changed?

From a pair to a lattice

Ions do not pair off one to one. Each ion attracts every oppositely charged neighbour, so they stack into a repeating crystal: a lattice.

Not every salt packs the same way. How the ions stack depends on their sizes: Cs⁺ is much bigger than Na⁺, so in CsCl each ion fits 8 neighbours around it instead of 6.

Build each salt step by step, and compare NaCl with CsCl. Drag to turn the crystal, and click an ion to see which ions touch it.

Salts side by side

Melting a salt means pulling its ions apart. Compare the melting points: what changes when the ions get bigger? What changes when the charges double?

Lattice energy: the energy needed to pull the ions of a crystal apart. The bigger the lattice energy, the higher the melting point.

LE = kq₁q₂r

Here q₁ and q₂ are the charges on the two ions, r is the distance between their centres, and k is a constant.

Network and molecular crystals sit on the same scale as a preview. You will meet them properly later in the course.

Switch between the comparisons and talk about what changes.

Chapter B·1: Lewis structures

Lewis structures help us determine how atoms in a molecule are organized, share electrons, and will start to let us understand their shape and most importantly, how they interact with one another.

In this section, we will learn about drawing shared electron pairs, structures, multiple bonds, formal charge, ions, resonance, and expanded octets.

Before we begin

Read what Chapters B·1 to B·3 are aiming for: Lewis structures, shape and polarity.

Learning intentions

  • We will learn how to represent covalent compounds in multiple ways
  • We will learn how shape and electronegativity affect the polarity of a molecule
  • We will use concepts such as VSEPR and formal charge to check our understanding of our Lewis structures

Curricular competencies

  • Questioning and predicting: I can hypothesize how changes in bond polarity or molecular shape might affect overall molecular polarity, proposing more than one possible configuration or outcome.
  • Processing and analyzing data and information: I can construct accurate Lewis structures, molecular geometry diagrams (using VSEPR theory), and models to represent covalent compounds, and analyze them to predict molecular shape and, eventually, bonding patterns.
  • Evaluating: I can evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of different molecular representations (e.g., Lewis structures vs. 3D VSEPR models) in explaining polarity and molecular behavior.
  • Communicating: I can clearly communicate the relationship between molecular shape, electronegativity, and polarity using scientific conventions, correct terminology, and appropriate visual representations.

The count

Every Lewis structure starts with one important number: the total number of valence electrons for the entire molecule. We get this number by adding all of the valence electrons for the entire structure.

From there, we can start determining how the atoms will share electrons in their structures to complete the octet (most of the time).

The general rule to start: each atom needs 8 electrons in its valence shell, hydrogen only needs two. We try to arrange the valence electrons available to “satisfy all octets”.

Pick a molecule: add up its valence electrons, then see where they all go in the structure.

Single bonds

Start with the skeleton: drag the atoms in from the tray and put the least electronegative atom in the middle. Click each atom to give it its valence electrons. Then drag an electron onto a neighbour’s electron: the two are shared, and the pair becomes a bond.

Build CH₄ and H₂O on the same tray. A small number above an atom counts how many more electrons it needs to complete its octet (two for hydrogen). When the numbers are gone, every atom is satisfied.

When the count runs short

Sometimes, in order to fulfil the octet, more than one pair of electrons needs to be shared. These are known as double or triple bonds.

Notice that from the perspective of each atom, their octet is completed.

Formal charge

Formal charge compares the electrons an atom started with to the electrons it has in the structure:

FC = valence − nonbonding − ½(bonding)

Both CO₂ arrangements give every atom an octet. Hover over each atom to compare their formal charges. The structure with formal charges closest to zero is the better one.

When charges can't all be zero (like in an ion), the better structure puts the negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom.

Switch between CO₂, HCN vs HNC and the cyanate ion, OCN⁻. Hover over each atom to read its formal charge.

Practice: multiple bonds

Draw all five on the tray. CO is the stretch: C has four valence electrons and O has six, yet both can reach an octet. Check the formal charges in the tally once you get there.

Polyatomic ions

An ion has gained or lost electrons. Build as usual, then click to add an extra electron, or use Erase to take one away. Once every atom is satisfied, the structure goes in brackets with the overall charge outside.

Resonance

Sometimes the double bond could sit in more than one equivalent place. Each drawing is a resonance structure. The real molecule is a blend of all of them.

Moving between the drawings, no atoms move: only electron pairs. A lone pair swings in to make a new bond, and the π pair of the old double bond moves out onto another atom. Curved arrows show where each pair goes.

Watch the electron pairs move from form to form, then show the hybrid: the real molecule.

Expanded octets

Atoms from period 3 and below can hold more than eight electrons around them. Build PCl₅ and SF₆ and watch the count go past 8.

Practice: ions and mixed

Draw all five on the tray, mixing ions, resonance and an expanded octet.

Now practice on paper

Now we put the tray away and draw on paper. For each one, count the valence electrons, place the bonds, then add lone pairs until every atom is satisfied. Check the formal charges where an atom could go more than one way.

Chapter B·2: Shape (VSEPR: Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion)

How electron domains set the shape of a molecule.

Electron domains spread out

Electron groups around a central atom repel each other, so they spread as far apart as possible. That spread sets the shape. This is VSEPR.

Step through AX₂ to AX₆ (A = the central atom, X = an atom bonded to it) and watch each flat drawing lift into its 3D shape. Drag to turn it, and try Space filling.

Lone pairs bend shapes

Lone pairs take up space too. CH₄, NH₃ and H₂O all have four domains, but each lone pair squeezes the bond angle a little more.

Step from CH₄ to NH₃ to H₂O and watch the angle shrink. The yellow lobes are lone pairs.

Five and six domains

Expanded octets bring five and six domains: trigonal bipyramidal and octahedral, plus the shapes left when some domains are lone pairs.

Click a card to see that shape in 3D. Drag to turn it.

Practice: name the shape

Count the domains and lone pairs, then choose the shape.

Chapter B·3: Polarity

How bond polarity and shape combine.

Bond dipoles add up by shape

Each polar bond pulls electrons one way. If the shape makes those pulls cancel, the molecule is nonpolar overall. If not, it is polar.

Pick a molecule: the bond dipoles appear, then join tip to tail to give the net dipole.

What if…

Swap one Cl in CCl₄ for H. Change BF₃ into NH₃. Predict first, then check.

Chapter C: Hybridization

How carbon’s s and p orbitals combine, and the angles that follow.

Before we begin

Read what this chapter is aiming for.

Learning intentions

  • We will review how “hybridization” can let us predict the shape of a bonding atom within a molecule
  • We will connect the type of hybridization with the common angles seen in molecules

Curricular competencies

  • Questioning and predicting: I can predict the hybridization of a carbon atom from the number of electron domains around it, and use it to predict the bond angles around that atom.
  • Processing and analyzing data and information: I can use orbital diagrams to show how carbon’s 2s and 2p orbitals combine into sp³, sp² or sp orbitals, and connect each one to its angle (about 109.5°, 120° or 180°).
  • Evaluating: I can evaluate what a Lewis structure, an orbital diagram and a 3D model each show and hide about the bonds and shape around a carbon atom.
  • Communicating: I can label each carbon in a molecule as sp³, sp² or sp and explain my choice using correct terminology, such as hybrid orbital, σ bond, π bond and bond angle.

Carbon’s puzzle

Carbon’s ground state, 2s² 2p², has only two unpaired electrons. Yet methane has four identical C–H bonds, 109.5° apart.

Promoting an electron gives four unpaired electrons, but the orbitals still do not fit: the three 2p orbitals are 90° apart, and 2s points nowhere. Combining all four into sp³ orbitals gives four identical orbitals at 109.5°.

Step through: ground state, promote, check the angles, combine. Drag the model to turn it.

Ethane: sp³

Both carbons in ethane are sp³. A bond forms where two orbitals overlap and their two electrons pair up. One sp³ orbital from each carbon overlaps head on to make the C–C σ bond. Each remaining sp³ orbital overlaps a hydrogen 1s orbital to make a C–H σ bond.

Step 1 shows two sp³ carbons, step 2 joins them, step 3 adds the hydrogens. Drag the model to turn it.

Ethene: sp²

In ethene, each carbon combines 2s with two 2p orbitals: three sp² orbitals, 120° apart in a plane. The leftover p orbitals overlap side by side to make the second bond, a π bond. You drew H₂CCH₂ in practice.

Step through the same three steps for ethene. Look for the π bond in step 3.

Ethyne: sp

In ethyne, 2s combines with one 2p: two sp orbitals, 180° apart. Two leftover p orbitals make two π bonds.

Step through for ethyne: two π bonds this time.

The pattern

Four domains: sp³, about 109.5°. Three domains: sp², about 120°. Two domains: sp, 180°.

Click a row to see where its orbitals come from.
