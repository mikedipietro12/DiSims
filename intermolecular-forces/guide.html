Introduction

You already know bonds inside a molecule. Intermolecular forces are attractions between molecules. They are weaker than chemical bonds, but they decide what types of substances stick, melt, and boil in certain conditions.

One molecule

Start with a single hydrogen chloride molecule. The white sphere is hydrogen; the green sphere is chlorine. These are the atoms: the nuclei and their tightly held core electrons, drawn as a model-kit toy.

Intro · 2 of 5

The bond inside

Atoms in a molecule share electrons in a covalent bond. That bond is an intramolecular force: it holds the molecule together from the inside. It is much stronger than anything you will see between molecules later.

  • Watch: we zoom into the bond. The two dots are a shared electron pair.
  • Key word: intra = inside the molecule.

Intro · 3 of 5

Electron clouds

Valence electrons are not stuck on one atom. They zip around the nuclei so fast that, on average, they form a soft cloud. On this tray the cloud is the fuzzy halo. For now it is even and neutral; we have not yet said which atom pulls the electrons more.

  • Watch: some electrons loop around Cl; others trace figure-8s through the bond, visiting both atoms. Those are the shared electrons.

Intro · 4 of 5

Electronegativity

Electronegativity (EN) is how strongly an atom pulls bonding electrons toward itself. Chlorine’s EN is higher than hydrogen’s, so the shared cloud is tugged toward Cl. That uneven pull shows up as colour: yellow near Cl, red near H. For the rest of these simulations, yellow will always represent a negatively charged area, and red will represent a positively charged area.

  • Watch: the cloud shifts colour, EN labels on each atom, and the arrow toward the stronger pull.
  • Numbers: H ≈ 2.1 · Cl ≈ 3.0 (Pauling scale).

Intro · 5 of 5

Polarity

Uneven sharing leaves a permanent imbalance: the H end is slightly electron-poor (δ+), the Cl end slightly electron-rich (δ−). That is a permanent dipole: the molecule is polar.

Bridge

Between molecules

Bonds and polarity live inside each molecule. Intermolecular forces act when two particles get close: attractions (and sometimes pushes) between them. Weaker than covalent bonds, but they decide what types of substances stick, melt, and boil in certain conditions.

Drag one HCl toward the others and let go. As they meet, each one's δ+ end turns toward a neighbour's δ− end. Temporary polarity from London dispersion will pulse later. Permanent polarity stays fixed like this.

  • Try: drag the molecules together, then Turn A / Turn B to feel orientation.
  • Remember: bonds hold atoms inside; IMFs act between molecules.

Force 1 · London dispersion

London dispersion

Electrons are always moving. For a moment, one side of a molecule can be slightly richer in electrons than the other: a temporary dipole. That flicker can nudge a neighbour into a matching temporary dipole, and the two pull gently together.

Every molecule has London dispersion. It gets stronger when the electron cloud is bigger or more polarizable, and when molecules have more contact area.

  • Stronger when: larger / more polarizable clouds, more surface contact (e.g. Br₂ or octane vs CH₄).
  • Weaker when: tiny clouds and little contact (Ne, CH₄).
  • Watch for: pulsing temporary δ±, not fixed polarity colour.

For molecules of similar size, permanent dipole-dipole is usually stronger than LDF. Big nonpolar molecules can still stick hard via LDF alone.

Force 2 · Dipole-induced dipole

Dipole-induced dipole

A polar molecule already has a permanent δ+ / δ−. Bring it near a nonpolar neighbour and that fixed dipole can induce a temporary dipole in the neighbour’s cloud. It is always attractive, usually soft.

You still see some LDF-style pulse on the nonpolar partner; the polar molecule’s permanent charges stay fixed.

  • Needs: one polar + one nonpolar (or weakly polarizable) partner.
  • Stronger when: larger permanent dipole, more polarizable nonpolar partner.

Force 3 · Ion-induced dipole

Ion-induced dipole

An ion’s full charge is much stronger than a partial δ. Near a nonpolar molecule it pulls electron density toward itself, inducing a temporary dipole. The cloud leans, and the pair attracts.

Same “distort the neighbour” idea as dipole-induced, but the ion is the more forceful inducer. Na⁺ still shows a real “+”, not a δ.

  • Needs: ion + nonpolar molecule.
  • Stronger when: higher ion charge density, more polarizable nonpolar cloud.

Force 4 · Dipole-dipole

Dipole-dipole

Acetone has a strongly polar C=O group: δ− on the oxygen, δ+ on the carbon. Neighbouring acetones line up so δ+ faces δ−. Opposite partial charges pull; like charges push. Orientation matters. Turn a molecule and the pull can weaken or flip to a push.

Acetone has no O–H, so there is no hydrogen bonding between acetones. Dipole-dipole does the work. Use Turn A / Turn B to feel how alignment changes the interaction.

  • Needs: two polar molecules; no H-bond donor required.
  • Stronger when: larger permanent dipoles, favourable orientation.
  • Caveat: compared at similar molecular size, big LDF can still compete.

Force 5 · Ion-dipole

Ion-dipole

An ion next to a polar molecule is usually a strong attraction. Each water’s δ− oxygen end orients toward Na⁺, so the waters gather round the ion with their oxygens facing in.

This is typically stronger than ordinary dipole-dipole: full charge meets a permanent dipole.

  • Needs: ion + polar molecule.
  • Stronger when: higher ion charge, larger molecular dipole, closer approach.

Force 6 · Hydrogen bonding

Hydrogen bonding

A special, strong case of attraction: a hydrogen bonded to N, O, or F (the donor) docks onto the lone-pair electrons of N, O, or F on a neighbour (the acceptor). The H is attracted to those valence electrons, drawn here as yellow lobes with electron-pair dots.

Favourable geometry is roughly linear: donor-H···acceptor. Small molecules like water usually settle into one primary H-bond at a time on this tray.

  • Needs: H bonded to N/O/F, plus an N/O/F acceptor with a lone pair.
  • Stronger when: more H-bond sites (e.g. glucose), good alignment, highly electronegative acceptor.
  • Watch for: yellow lone-pair lobes. That is where the δ+ H docks.

Names in your pocket

Typical strength order for similar-sized cases:

ion-dipole → hydrogen bonding → ion-induced → dipole-dipole → dipole-induced → London dispersion
