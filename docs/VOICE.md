# VOICE.md — how student-facing copy should sound

Read this before writing or editing any text a student will read (Guide sections, briefings, hints,
captions, cards). It is built from the teacher's own rewrites. AGENTS.md "Student-facing copy" rules
still apply on top of this.

## Rules (confirmed)

A pattern moves here once it shows up in more than one edit and the teacher agrees.

1. **Teacher's "we"** (confirmed 2026-11-19). Talk as the class: "we learned", "we will review", "we write it",
   "one way we use Ksp". Not "you met these" or "you will see". Instructions to students can still say what to
   click ("Click the “Next step” button").

## Patterns seen so far (not yet rules)

2. **Ties to last year in plain words.** "Last year, we learned about..." rather than naming the course
   ("Chemistry 11").
3. **Says why the section matters.** An opening line names what we're doing and why: "...as they will be
   important tools moving forward."
4. **Labelled lines, not paragraphs.** Key ideas as a bold label and a colon, one idea per line:
   **Concentration:**, **Dilution:**, **Recall:**, then "For example:".
5. **Short definitions; the method lives in the sim.** States what the idea *is* in one sentence and leaves
   the how-to (e.g. unit-analysis instructions) to the stepped calculation on the stage.
6. **Tells students what to do with the sim.** Ends by pointing at the stage and the control to press:
   "Click the "Next step" button to see how the calculations occur as we go from grams, to moles, to
   concentration."
7. **Plain connectives.** "Therefore", "that means". Everyday wording over compact technical phrasing
   ("the litres change, but the moles don't").

### 2026-11-18 · Solubility Guide, Part 1 "A quick look back: concentration and dilution"

Before:
> You met these in Chemistry 11. **Concentration** tells you how much solute is in each litre of solution,
> in mol/L (also written M). Work it out with unit analysis: write each value as a fraction with its units,
> and choose conversion factors so the units you don't want cancel.
>
> **Dilution** adds water but not solute. The moles stay the same but spread through more litres, so the
> concentration drops by the same factor the volume grows.
>
> One more idea you will need all unit. When an ionic compound dissolves, each ion's concentration depends
> on how many of that ion are in the formula. 0.10 M Pb(NO₃)₂ gives [Pb²⁺] = 0.10 M, but [NO₃⁻] = 0.20 M.

After:
> Last year, we learned about concentration and dilution. In this section, we will review both of those
> concepts, as they will be important tools moving forward.
>
> **Concentration:** tells you how much solute is dissolved in a given volume of water (mol/L).
>
> **Dilution:** adds water to a solution which already has something dissolved in it. Therefore the litres
> change, but the moles don't.
>
> **Recall:** When an ionic compound dissolves, each ion's concentration depends on how many of that ion are
> in the chemical formula.
> For example, in PbI₂: if we had a concentration of 0.1 M, that means we would have 0.1 M Pb²⁺ and 0.2 M I⁻.
>
> On the right, we will see a quick review of making a solution in the lab, as we did last year, as well as
> diluting a solution. Click the "Next step" button to see how the calculations occur as we go from grams,
> to moles, to concentration.

Light fixes made when applying it: "ions" → "ion's", "0.2M" → "0.2 M", sub/superscripts on formulas.
Then, with the teacher's OK: "volume of water" → "volume of solution"; PbI₂ example → Pb(NO₃)₂ (PbI₂ can't
reach 0.1 M); "On the right" → "In the simulation" (the stage is above the text on a portrait tablet);
the bullet list under the section was cut because the closing line already points to the sim.

Content checks to keep doing on the teacher's wording (fix with their OK, keep the voice):
- Chemistry has to be right for *this* unit: no example concentrations a salt can't reach.
- Concentration is per volume of **solution**, not water.
- Don't name screen positions ("on the right"); say "in the simulation".
- Use curly quotes “ ” in student text; straight " renders as a backwards opening quote in the site font.

### 2026-11-19 · Solubility Guide, Part 1 Ksp section

Before:
> **Ksp: the same K, for a solid dissolving**
> A saturated solution is at equilibrium, so it has a K. For a solid dissolving, it is called the **solubility
> product constant**, **Ksp**.
> Write it like any K: products over reactants. The solid is left out, just as pure solids were left out of K
> before. That leaves only the ions, each raised to its coefficient.
> Three numbers describe how much dissolves. The **solubility** is the mass that dissolves, in g/L. The **molar
> solubility**, **s**, is the moles of compound that dissolve, in mol/L. **Ksp** comes from the ion concentrations.
> **Notice:** a small Ksp means only a little dissolves. Every Ksp in the booklet is much less than 1.

After:
> **What is Ksp?**
> A saturated solution is at equilibrium, so it has a K value associated with it. For solids dissolving, we have a
> specific K value, called Ksp, or “Solubility Product”.
> We write it like any K: products over reactants. As a reminder: the solid is left out, just as pure solids were
> left out of K before. That leaves only the ions, each raised to its coefficient, again, the PRODUCT of their
> SOLUBILITY.
> At this point, there are three numbers for us to derive the Ksp.
> Solubility (g/L)
> Molar Solubility of the salt (mol/L)
> Solubility of each Ion (mol/L)
> The HIGHER the Ksp, the more PRODUCTS are favored, therefore, the more soluble the salt is.
> The LOWER the Ksp, the more REACTANTS are favored, the less soluble the salt is.

Light fixes made when applying it: curly quotes, Ksp subscript, "favored" → "favoured" (the site uses Canadian
spelling), "Molar Solubility" / "Ion" lower-cased, list labels in bold to match the labelled-line pattern.
Kept as written: the CAPITALS for emphasis.

New patterns seen here (first time each): **question titles** ("What is Ksp?" over a statement title);
**CAPITALS for the key words** in a rule (HIGHER / PRODUCTS / LOWER / REACTANTS); **paired contrast lines**
(the HIGHER…, the LOWER…). Repeated from the first rewrite: teacher "we", labelled lines ("As a reminder:"
like "Recall:"), "therefore", the "Notice" bullet replaced by the teacher's own closing lines.

### 2026-11-20 · Solubility Guide, Part 3 "The Common Ion Effect" (`p3-common`)

Before:
> **The common ion effect**
>
> Add KI to saturated PbI₂. KI is soluble, so it adds lots of I⁻. That raises Q above Ksp, and the equilibrium
> shifts left. Yellow PbI₂ forms until Q = Ksp again.
>
> An ion that is already part of the equilibrium is called a **common ion**. Adding a common ion always makes a
> salt **less** soluble. This is the **common ion effect**.
> ...
> How much less soluble? Work out the molar solubility of PbI₂ in 0.10 M KI.

After:
> **The Common Ion Effect**
>
> An ion that is already part of the equilibrium is called a **common ion**.
> Adding a **common ion** always makes a salt **less** soluble.
> This is the **common ion effect**.
>
> Often, that common ion is added in the form of a salt that is very soluble (e.g. KI).
> ...
> How much less soluble? We can calculate the new solubility using the same ICE table principles from before.

The mechanism paragraph went (the Watch lines and the sim carry it); definition first, one idea per line
(pattern 4); "we can calculate" (rule 1); points back to the method already learned (ICE table) instead of an
instruction. Title case again (second time, after "Stressing a Saturated Solution"): likely a pattern for
section headings. Light fixes: "eg." → "e.g.", "ice table principals" → "ICE table principles". The worked steps
gained an ICE table (I row: 0 and 0.10) so the copy's promise is true.

### 2026-11-24 · Solubility Guide, Part 4 “Reading the experiment” (`p4-scheme`) opening (my wording, not yet reviewed)

The teacher asked for the task to be set up first: what ions could be there, why we remove them, how. I wrote it in the
labelled-line pattern (4):
> In our lab, each group gets an **unknown**: a colourless solution that may contain any mix of Pb²⁺, Sr²⁺ and Mg²⁺, or none
> of them. Our task is to find out which of these ions are in it.
> **The problem:** one reagent can make a solid with more than one ion. Na₂SO₄ gives a white solid with Pb²⁺ and with Sr²⁺. If
> both are still in the tube, a white solid doesn't tell us which one is there.
> **The solution:** we test for one ion at a time, and remove each ion before the next test. We add a reagent that makes only one
> ion precipitate, then filter the solid out. That ion is now gone, so it can't form a solid in a later test.
> **The order:** KI tests for Pb²⁺ · Na₂SO₄ tests for Sr²⁺ · NaOH tests for Mg²⁺ (each with the solid and its colour)
Then the filter / filtrate steps as one line, and the closing “Click the “Next step” button…” line (pattern 6).
