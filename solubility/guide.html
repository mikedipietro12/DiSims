A quick look back: concentration and dilution

Last year, we learned about concentration and dilution. In this section, we will review both of those concepts, as they will be important tools moving forward.

Concentration: tells you how much solute is dissolved in a given volume of solution (mol/L).

Dilution: adds water to a solution which already has something dissolved in it. Therefore the litres change, but the moles don't.

Recall: When an ionic compound dissolves, each ion's concentration depends on how many of that ion are in the chemical formula.
For example, in Pb(NO3)2: if we had a concentration of 0.1 M, that means we would have 0.1 M Pb2+ and 0.2 M NO3−.

In the simulation, we will see a quick review of making a solution in the lab, as we did last year, as well as diluting a solution. Click the “Next step” button to see how the calculations occur as we go from grams, to moles, to concentration.

Solubility rules review

The Data Booklet's solubility table sorts ionic compounds into two groups. Soluble means more than 0.1 mol/L dissolves at 25 °C. Everything else has low solubility.

In the simulation, choose soluble or low solubility for each compound. The card turns green for a correct answer and red for an incorrect one. Find the rules in your Chemistry Data Booklet on page 4.

  • Notice: “low solubility” is not “zero”. The table only tells us which side of 0.1 mol/L a compound is on. It cannot tell us how much dissolves.

“Insoluble” still dissolves a little

Add AgCl to water and stir. Almost all of it sits on the bottom, but a tiny amount does dissolve. Once the water holds as much as it can, the solution is saturated.

Zoom in on a saturated solution. Ions leave the crystal surface and other ions land back on it, at the same rate. The amount of solid stops changing, but both processes keep going. This is the same dynamic equilibrium we saw with K.

  • Watch: one scoop dissolves completely. The second scoop leaves solid behind, and the “Dissolved” value stops rising.
  • Watch: in the window, an ion leaves the crystal and another one lands, again and again.

What is Ksp?

A saturated solution is at equilibrium, so it has a K value associated with it. For solids dissolving, we have a specific K value, called Ksp, or “Solubility Product”.

We write it like any K: products over reactants. As a reminder: the solid is left out, just as pure solids were left out of K before. That leaves only the ions, each raised to its coefficient, again, the PRODUCT of their SOLUBILITY.

At this point, there are three numbers for us to derive the Ksp.
Solubility (g/L)
Molar solubility of the salt (mol/L)
Solubility of each ion (mol/L)

g/L⇄÷ molar mass mol/L⇄× ion counts [ions]⇄into Ksp Ksp

The HIGHER the Ksp, the more PRODUCTS are favoured, therefore, the more soluble the salt is.
The LOWER the Ksp, the more REACTANTS are favoured, the less soluble the salt is.

From Ksp to solubility: lead(II) iodide

PbI2 is different. Each formula unit gives one Pb2+ but two I−. Before we start, predict.

  • Watch: the window's ion counts. There are always twice as many I− as Pb2+. That is where the 4 in 4x3 comes from: (2x)2 = 4x2.

A common slip is to write [I⁻] = x. That gives Ksp = x³ and a molar solubility about 1.6 times too big.

Working backwards: from a measurement to Ksp

In the lab, we measure something and work back to Ksp. There is more than one way to do it.

Strategy 1: mass. Evaporate a known volume of saturated solution and weigh what is left. 250.0 mL of saturated Mg(OH)2 leaves 1.63 mg of solid.

Strategy 2: conductivity. Dissolved ions let a solution carry an electric current. The more ions, the higher the conductivity. A conductivity probe can't name the ions, but it can tell us how many there are. First, measure standards of known concentration and plot a calibration line: conductivity against concentration. Then measure the saturated solution and read its concentration off the line.

Strategy 3: a pH probe. We add Mg(OH)2 to neutral water a little at a time and watch the pH. Once the pH stops rising, the solution is saturated. From the pH we find [OH−], and [Mg2+] is half of that. (pH and pOH are next unit. This is a preview.)

Every strategy depends on careful measurement. Record the volume in litres, keep the mass in grams, use distilled water (other ions would add to the conductivity), and keep the right number of significant figures. A small error in s grows in Ksp, because s is cubed.

  • Notice: working backwards uses the same arrows as before, in the other direction.
  • Notice: three different measurements, one Ksp. If two strategies disagree, check the measurements before the algebra.

  • We will connect Q and K back to equilibrium and how it applies to salts
  • We will begin our next lab!

Curricular competencies

  • Processing and analyzing data and information: We will use our knowledge of scientific concepts to draw conclusions that are consistent with evidence in the lab

Q: the same expression, at any moment

Ksp uses the ion concentrations at equilibrium. The ion product, Q, uses the same expression with the concentrations right now, whatever they are.

Comparing Q with Ksp tells us what happens next. This is the same Q vs K reading we already know.

  • Q < Ksp: the solution is unsaturated. More solid can dissolve.
  • Q = Ksp: the solution is saturated. Nothing changes overall.
  • Q > Ksp: too many ions for the water to hold. A solid precipitates (forms and settles out) until Q falls back to Ksp.
  • Watch: add PbI2 one scoop at a time. The Q marker climbs towards Ksp and stops there once solid stays on the bottom.

Mixing two solutions

When two solutions are poured together, each one spreads into the total volume. Every concentration drops before anything else happens. We work out the new concentrations first, then Q.

In the simulation, 50.0 mL of 0.010 M Pb(NO3)2 is mixed with 50.0 mL of 0.020 M KI. Click the “Next step” button to pour them together, write the net ionic equation, and build Q one step at a time. Then we compare Q with Ksp to see if a solid forms.

Will a solid form?

Now with unequal volumes. Predict, then check: 25.0 mL of 0.010 M AgNO3 is mixed with 75.0 mL of 0.0010 M NaCl.

Then try four quick ones. Mix 1 and Mix 2 use equal volumes, so every concentration halves. Mix 3 and Mix 4 use different volumes, so we work out each new concentration first. Choose Yes or No (the card turns green for a correct answer and red for an incorrect one), then press Mix to run the real thing.

  • Notice: the spectator ions (Na+, K+, NO3−) never appear in Q. The solubility rules from Part 1 say they stay dissolved.

Back to equilibrium: Q moves to Ksp

When Q > Ksp, the solid does not form all at once and then stop at a random point. Ions join the solid until the ions left in the water give Q = Ksp exactly. The solution left above the solid is saturated.

It is the same story as the Le Châtelier sim. When Q > K, the reaction runs in reverse until Q = K. Here, “in reverse” means ions leaving the water and joining the solid.

  • Watch: after the AgCl mix, the Q marker slides back from off the scale to the Ksp line. In the window, new layers build up on the solid.
  • Watch: the arrows in the dissolving equation. The reverse arrow is bigger while the solid forms, and both arrows are equal once Q = Ksp.
  • Notice: the dissolved ions do not drop to zero. In the AgCl mix there was more Ag+ than Cl−. Once Q = Ksp, about 1.75 × 10−3 M Ag+ is left, and only 1.0 × 10−7 M Cl−. Almost all the chloride is now in the solid. Part 4 uses exactly this to separate ions.

What does the evidence show?

In the lab, we see a result, not Q. Our conclusion has to match what we actually saw. For each test tube, choose the conclusion that fits the observation.

  • Notice: a good lab conclusion states the observation, links it to Q and Ksp, and says whether it agrees with the prediction.

Stressing a Saturated Solution

A saturated solution with solid on the bottom is at equilibrium. That means Le Châtelier's principle applies. If we add a stress, the equilibrium shifts to reduce it.

For PbI2(s) ⇌ Pb2+ + 2 I− there are three stresses we can apply:

  • add one of the ions
  • remove one of the ions
  • change the temperature

A shift to the right dissolves more solid. A shift to the left forms more solid.

  • Notice: adding more solid is not a stress. The solid is not in the Ksp expression, so the concentrations stay the same. There is just more on the bottom.
  • Notice: only temperature changes the value of Ksp. The other two stresses change Q, and the system shifts until Q = Ksp again.

The Common Ion Effect

An ion that is already part of the equilibrium is called a common ion.
Adding a common ion always makes a salt less soluble.
This is the common ion effect.

Often, that common ion is added in the form of a salt that is very soluble (e.g. KI).

  • Watch: press Add KI. The Q marker jumps past Ksp, new layers form on the solid, and the marker slides back to Ksp.
  • Watch: after the shift, there is more I− than before but less Pb2+. The window shows fewer Pb2+ ions.

How much less soluble? We can calculate the new solubility using the same ICE table principles from before.

Which lowers the solubility of PbI2 more: 0.10 M KI or 0.10 M Pb(NO3)2?

The same idea works for any salt. AgCl in 0.10 M NaCl has s = 1.8 × 10−9 mol/L, about 7500 times less than in pure water.

Removing an ion: acid

Some anions react with H+.
Recall that: OH− and H+ make water. CO32− and H+ make HCO3−.
If acid removes the anion from a saturated solution, Q drops below Ksp.
The equilibrium shifts right and more solid dissolves. We saw this during our kinetics lab when we reacted seashells with hydrochloric acid, making the seashells much more soluble.

Remember the milk of magnesia card from Part 1? Mg(OH)2 is a white solid with very low solubility. Stomach acid removes its OH−, so it keeps dissolving. That is how an antacid works.

  • Watch: add acid to saturated Mg(OH)2. The window zooms in where the acid acts. H+ meets OH− to make H2O, then Mg2+ and OH− leave the crystal, and the top layer gets smaller.
  • Watch: each press of Add acid adds 1.0 × 10−3 M acid. After one press, Mg(OH)2 dissolves about 5 times more than in pure water, and CaCO3 about 14 times more.

Adding acid makes which of these dissolve more: AgCl, Mg(OH)2, PbI2 or CaCO3?

Calcium carbonate works the same way. Rainwater picks up CO2 and becomes slightly acidic. Over thousands of years it dissolves limestone and carves out caves. Seawater is becoming slightly more acidic too, which makes it harder for shells made of CaCO3 to form.

  • Notice: adding a base does the opposite to Mg(OH)2. NaOH adds OH−, which is a common ion, so less dissolves.

Changing the temperature

For most salts, dissolving takes in heat: ΔH is positive. Treat heat as a reactant:

heat + PbI2(s) ⇌ Pb2+ + 2 I−

Heating adds a “reactant”, so the equilibrium shifts right and more dissolves. Cooling shifts it left and solid forms. Temperature is the only stress that changes the value of Ksp.

  • Watch: heat the beaker. The solid shrinks and the Ksp mark itself moves on the check, not just Q.
  • Watch: cool it again. Bright yellow PbI2 crystals form as the solution cools. Chemists call this “golden rain”.
  • Notice: about 6.6 times more PbI2 dissolves at 100 °C than at 25 °C. The Data Booklet's Ksp values are all for 25 °C.

Predict, then test

Use Le Châtelier to predict each change. Write a hypothesis in the form “If I…, then…, because…”. Then test each one in the Sandbox.

  • Notice: every answer comes from one question: does the change raise Q, lower Q, or change Ksp?

What's in the water?

Qualitative analysis finds out which ions are in a sample, not how much. (Finding how much is quantitative analysis, like our Ksp lab.)

The idea is simple. Add a reagent that forms a solid with one ion and not the others. If a solid appears, that ion was there. Then separate the solid and test the liquid that's left for the next ion.

  • Notice: a first observation (colour, smell, where the sample came from) narrows the list before any test. That is a hypothesis, not a result yet.

One reagent, one solid

Selective precipitation means choosing a reagent that makes only one of the ions precipitate. The solubility rules from Part 1 tell us which reagent to try.

Ksp shows how well it works. With 0.050 M Pb2+, a solid starts once [I−] reaches 4.1 × 10−4 M. The scheme adds plenty of KI, so [I−] ends up near 0.9 M. Then the Pb2+ left in solution is

[Pb2+] = Ksp ÷ [I−]2 = 8.5 × 10−9 ÷ (0.9)2 ≈ 1 × 10−8 M

That is less than one millionth of what was there. The Pb2+ has been isolated in the solid.

  • Watch: in the window, Pb2+ joins the yellow solid while Sr2+ and Mg2+ stay dissolved.
  • Notice: this is Part 2's Q vs Ksp and Part 3's common ion effect, used on purpose. The extra I− pushes almost all the Pb2+ out.

Why test for Pb2+ first?

Suppose we skip iodide and add Na2SO4 first. Both PbSO4 and SrSO4 have low solubility. Which one forms first? Step through it with 0.050 M of each ion.

The two Ksp values are too close, and both solids are white. We couldn't tell which ion made the solid. So the scheme takes Pb2+ out first with iodide, which leaves Sr2+ alone. Then sulphate finds only Sr2+.

Reading a scheme

A scheme is a flowchart for an unknown. At each step:

  1. Add the reagent.
  2. Look for a solid.
  3. Centrifuge (spin the tube so the solid packs at the bottom).
  4. Decant (pour the liquid into a clean tube).
  5. Take the liquid to the next step.

The example unknown on the stage contains Pb2+ and Mg2+. Step through it.

  • Notice: “stays clear” is evidence too. It tells us an ion is absent, provided the earlier steps removed anything that would interfere.

Who uses qualitative analysis?

Testing for ions is everyday work in many jobs. Today most labs use instruments as well as precipitation tests, but the reasoning is the same: a test, an observation, and a conclusion that fits the evidence.

Pick one career. What would they need to know about solubility? What question would you ask them?

This water may contain lead

An older school building has lead pipes in one wing. Someone asks whether we could test the tap water for Pb2+ with KI, the way the scheme does. Step through it: is a precipitation test enough?

So a clear tube does not prove the water is safe. Water at the limit would still look clear. The KI test can show a big problem, but it can't show the water meets the limit.
