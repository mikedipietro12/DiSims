Part 1 · Enthalpy

Up the hill, then down

You already know this one. Enthalpy (H) is the heat stored in a system. ΔH is how much it changes.

A potential energy diagram follows a reaction from left to right. To react, particles must collide hard enough to climb to the top of the hill, the activated complex. Past the top, they slide down to the products.

ΔH is the gap between where the reactants start and where the products end. If the products end lower, that energy leaves as heat and warms the surroundings. ΔH is negative: the change is exothermic. If the products end higher, the energy comes in as heat from the surroundings. ΔH is positive: the change is endothermic.

  • Watch: in Exothermic, A₂ and B₂ climb together. At the top, bonds break and new ones form, so the atoms re-pair as AB. Heat comes off as the products settle on the lower shelf.
  • Switch to Endothermic: the same hill, read backwards. AB re-pairs into A₂ and B₂, and heat flows in.
  • Notice: Eₐ and ΔH are different gaps. Eₐ is about how fast. ΔH is about how much heat.

Part 1 · Enthalpy

Where does the heat go?

Now we need to think about actual systems, and the flow of heat energy into or out of the system.

The sign tells us part of the story, but combined with our understanding of the PE diagrams, we can get a full picture of what is going on.

Recall: breaking bonds or pulling particles apart costs energy. Forming bonds or letting particles settle into a lower energy state releases energy.

  • ΔH + when more energy goes into breaking than comes out of forming. Melting and boiling are examples.
  • ΔH − when forming wins. Burning fuel and freezing are examples.
  • Watch: what changes in each demo? Use it to decide which way heat flows, then predict the sign of ΔH.
  • Heat is a term in the equation: when heat comes out, it is written on the products side. When heat goes in, it is written on the reactants side.

Physical changes like melting and chemical changes like burning both have a ΔH. The rule is the same for both.

Part 1 · Entropy

Let's talk about randomness

One of the ways we can talk about entropy is thinking about randomness. Or, things "spreading out". Think about dropping food colouring into water. It spreads out until the colour is even. It never gathers itself back into the drop. Entropy (S) measures how spread out, or random, a system's particles and energy are. The more random ways the particles can be arranged, the higher the entropy.

ΔS is the change. Spreading out makes ΔS positive. Getting more ordered or more crowded makes ΔS negative.

  • Solid → liquid → gas: each step makes ΔS more positive. A gas has by far the most freedom.
  • More gas particles: a reaction that makes more gas particles has ΔS +. N₂O₄ → 2NO₂ turns one particle into two.
  • Dissolving: usually ΔS +, as a neat crystal breaks up into ions that spread through the water.
  • Watch: the same particles before and after. Think about how many ways they could be arranged.

Rules of thumb have exceptions. Ca²⁺ is a small ion with a big charge, so it attracts water molecules strongly and binds them in a tight shell. Those trapped water molecules lose more freedom than the ions gain. Chemists check the data rather than trust the rule.

Part 1 · Enthalpy and entropy together

Two different pulls

ΔH and ΔS are not the same thing. ΔH is about heat. ΔS is about randomness. A change is favoured when it gives off heat (ΔH −), and also when a system gets more random, or spread out (ΔS +).

We will now look at systems and decide which direction entropy and enthalpy are "pointing", or which side is favoured. A cold pack fights. It takes in heat, which works against it, yet it still dissolves on its own. Spreading out wins.

  • Both favour it (ΔH −, ΔS +): it happens.
  • Both oppose it (ΔH +, ΔS −): it does not happen on its own.
  • They fight: the winner depends on temperature. That is Part 3.
  • Notice: step through the cold pack with the arrows under the picture. Watch which way each half of the ⇌ points.

Part 1 · Putting them together

Four ways the pulls can line up

Chemists combine ΔH and ΔS into one quantity, the Gibbs free energy change:

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

If ΔG is negative, the products are favoured.

If ΔG is positive, the reactants are favoured.

T is the temperature in kelvin, which is always positive.

Notice the minus sign: when ΔS is +, the entropy term (−TΔS) is negative, so it helps make ΔG negative.

Part 2 · Reversible reactions

Reactions that run both ways

Many reactions are reversible. As soon as products form, some of them react back into reactants. We write this with a double arrow: ⇌.

Here is N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ in a sealed vessel. N₂O₄ is colourless and NO₂ is brown. Watch the colour (what you would see in the lab), then watch the particles (what you would see if you could zoom in).

  • Macro: the colour darkens, then holds steady.
  • Micro: particles keep splitting and joining. They never stop.
  • Watch: each ring is one reaction event. Count them after the colour stops changing.

The bottle looks finished, but it is not. Both reactions are still happening at the same speed.

Part 2 · Rates and equilibrium

When the rates meet

At the start there is only N₂O₄, so the forward reaction is fast and the reverse is zero. As NO₂ builds up, the forward rate slows (fewer N₂O₄ left to split) and the reverse rate speeds up (more NO₂ to join).

Eventually the two rates are equal. From then on, every NO₂ made is matched by one used up. The amounts stop changing even though both reactions continue. This is dynamic equilibrium.

  • Amount graph: the lines curve, then go flat.
  • Rate graph: the forward line falls and the reverse line rises until they meet.
  • Equilibrium does not mean equal amounts. It means equal rates.
  • Try: start with products instead. The rates still meet, from the other side.

You already know rates from collision theory. Equilibrium is what happens when two opposite rates balance.

Part 2 · Everywhere

Equilibrium is all around you

A sealed soda bottle is a system at equilibrium. CO₂ gas above the drink dissolves into the liquid at the same rate as dissolved CO₂ escapes. The fizz stays put. Open the cap and the system is no longer closed, so the gas escapes faster than it returns.

The same balance shows up far from the chemistry lab.

  • Your blood: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ keeps your blood's acidity steady. Breathing faster removes CO₂ and shifts the balance.
  • The ocean: CO₂(g) ⇌ CO₂(aq) at the surface, then CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ in the water. More CO₂ in the air means more H⁺ in the sea: ocean acidification.
  • Notice: each one needs a closed system and two opposite processes running at the same rate.
  • Try: change a condition under the picture, watch the two arrows in the equation pull apart, then settle to equal again. Change it back and the system returns.

Where else might two opposite processes balance? Think about population in an ecosystem, or water evaporating and raining back down.

Part 3 · State functions

Only the start and finish matter

Climb a mountain by the steep trail or the long switchbacks. Either way your change in height is the same, because it depends only on where you started and where you finished. The distance you walked is different for each route.

H, S and G are state functions. Like height, their change depends only on the start and end states, not the path. That is why Hess's law works: add up any route of steps and you get the same ΔH. Heat and work are not state functions. They depend on the path, like the distance walked.

  • State functions: H, S, G (and height, temperature, pressure).
  • Path-dependent: heat, work (and distance walked).
  • Watch: two different routes between the same start and end give the same Δ.

Part 3 · Gibbs free energy

Temperature picks the winner

Combine the two pulls into one number, Gibbs free energy:

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

If ΔG is negative, the reaction goes forward on its own; we call it spontaneous. If ΔG is positive, the reverse is favoured. The T in the middle is the temperature in kelvin. The hotter it gets, the more the entropy pull (−TΔS) counts.

  • The bars: ΔH stays put. −TΔS stretches as you drag T. ΔG is where they land together.
  • When the pulls fight, dragging T across a certain point flips ΔG's sign.
  • Try: N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ (ΔH +, ΔS +). Cold, ΔG is +; hot, ΔG is −.
  • Try: Haber (ΔH −, ΔS −). It flips the other way.

"Spontaneous" does not mean fast or sudden. It only means "goes this way without being pushed". See the next beat.

Part 3 · Limits

What the formula leaves out

ΔG is powerful, but it is a model, and every model has limits.

It says nothing about speed. Diamond turning into graphite has ΔG −, so it is spontaneous at every temperature. It also takes millions of years. ΔG tells you which way a reaction can go. Rates (and collision theory) tell you how fast.

It assumes ΔH and ΔS stay fixed. Real values drift as temperature changes, so the flip temperature you find with ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is an estimate, not an exact answer.

  • Which way: ΔG.
  • How fast: rates and activation energy.
  • Notice: a "spontaneous" reaction can still need a spark to get started (think of a match).

Can you think of another reaction that "should" happen but doesn't seem to? (Paper burning in air is one.)

Before you leave

What to take with you

  • ΔH is heat flow. ΔS is spreading out. They are different pulls.
  • Many reactions run both ways. At equilibrium the rates are equal, and both directions keep going.
  • ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Temperature decides who wins when the pulls fight.
  • ΔG tells you which way, not how fast.
